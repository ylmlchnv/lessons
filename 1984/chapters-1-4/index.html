Stage 01

Where does control begin?

Choose a position quickly. The aim is not to find the “correct” answer; it is to make your assumptions visible before we return to Orwell.

10 min

Surveillance

“If you have nothing to hide, surveillance should not bother you.” Where do you draw the line between security and intrusion?

Truth

Can a government ever be justified in concealing or reshaping facts “for the public good”?

Conformity

If everyone around you publicly accepts the same belief, how difficult is it to preserve a private disagreement?

Language

Can changing the words available to us genuinely change the way we think, or only the way we express thought?

Bridge to the novel: Which of these four tensions is already most powerful in Chapters 1–4? Give one concrete moment from the text.
Stage 02

Four chapters, four lenses

Instead of retelling the plot, reduce each chapter to the new kind of control it reveals. Then test whether the label is too simple.

15 min
1

Private thought becomes dangerous

The telescreen, the Two Minutes Hate, the diary, Winston’s fear of his own face and gestures.

Discuss: Is the Party more interested in what Winston does, what he says, or what he feels?

2

Private life is penetrated

The Parsons household shows ideology entering the home through children, games, uniforms, suspicion, and political enthusiasm.

Discuss: Why are the children frightening precisely because they seem sincere?

3

Memory becomes political

Winston’s dreams, uncertain childhood memories, compulsory exercise, shifting alliances, and the problem of proving that the past was ever different.

Discuss: What happens to a person when memory is private but evidence is public?

4

Bureaucracy manufactures history

At the Ministry of Truth, Winston does not merely erase mistakes: he produces a past that fits the present, including the invented Comrade Ogilvy.

Discuss: Why might routine office work be more disturbing than obvious violence?

Stage 03

Historical context: what Orwell had already seen

Treat these as echoes, not as a one-to-one code. Oceania is fictional, but many of its methods draw on political systems and wartime institutions Orwell knew directly or observed closely.

15 min
1 · Stalin’s Soviet Union — terror, purges, and an editable past
Link to Ch. 3–4

Under Stalin, political repression, show trials, censorship, secret-police power and the removal of disgraced figures from public records created a culture in which official history could change with political necessity. In Orwell’s Ministry of Truth, this logic becomes an ordinary profession.

Question: Is Winston’s work primarily censorship, forgery, propaganda, or something more radical than all three?

2 · Nazi Germany — propaganda, mass emotion, and youth organisations
Link to Ch. 1–2

Nazi rule made extensive use of propaganda, public spectacle, leader worship, youth organisations and ideological education. The Hitler Youth and League of German Girls were designed to form loyal young citizens and subordinate individual identity to the regime.

Question: Compare that aim with the Parsons children. What happens when political loyalty becomes a source of excitement, status and play?

3 · Wartime Britain — propaganda, rationing, censorship, bureaucracy
Link to the whole opening world

Britain during the Second World War had rationing, bomb damage, government propaganda and a Ministry of Information responsible for publicity and censorship. Orwell himself worked for the BBC’s Eastern Service from 1941 to 1943, producing wartime broadcasts.

Question: Why is it important that Orwell’s satire also transforms familiar British institutions rather than locating political manipulation only “somewhere else”?

4 · Spain — propaganda and the fragility of a shared reality
Orwell’s political formation

Orwell fought on the Republican side in the Spanish Civil War and saw violent conflict within the anti-Franco camp, including Soviet-backed repression. He later wrote about encountering newspaper accounts that radically contradicted events he believed he had witnessed.

Question: How does that experience help explain Winston’s obsession with evidence and memory?

Useful distinction: Orwell was not simply “predicting smartphones.” He was asking what happens when a political system gains power over public evidence, language, emotion, habit and social trust.
Stage 04

Deep discussion

Choose four or five. For each answer, try to move from observation → interpretation → implication.

18 min

1 · Self-surveillance

Winston behaves as if he is being watched even when he cannot know whether anyone is watching. At what point does external surveillance become self-policing?

2 · Emotion as politics

During the Two Minutes Hate, people do not merely pretend to feel. Why is spontaneous emotion more useful to the Party than forced obedience?

3 · The diary

Why does writing privately feel like a political act? Is Winston trying to communicate, preserve evidence, preserve himself, or all three?

4 · The family

What happens to intimacy when children may become ideological observers of their parents? Which loyalties does the Party need to weaken?

5 · Winston’s complicity

He dislikes the system but performs highly skilled work for it. Does the novel present him as a rebel, a collaborator, a victim, or an unstable mixture?

6 · Ordinary bureaucracy

Why is the Ministry of Truth full of tubes, slips, cubicles, deadlines and procedural details? What does bureaucracy allow individuals not to think about?

7 · Memory vs evidence

If a memory cannot be externally verified, what gives it authority? Is private certainty enough to resist an official record?

8 · Scarcity

How might low-quality goods, shortages and physical discomfort contribute to political control even when they are not obviously “propaganda”?

Stage 06

Final debate

Build a case, then argue against yourself. Use evidence from at least two different chapters and one historical connection.

12 min
By the end of Chapter 4, the Party’s most frightening power is not physical violence but epistemic power: the ability to determine what can count as true.
epistemic = relating to knowledge, evidence, truth, and the conditions under which we can know something.

Build the case

  • What evidence supports the statement?
  • Why might control of records outlast individual acts of violence?
  • What role does Winston himself play in this system?

Attack the case

  • Could epistemic control exist without the threat of punishment?
  • Are fear and force actually the foundation of every other mechanism?
  • Which scene most strongly challenges the statement?

Language for literary discussion

Use these to make your ideas more precise, tentative and analytical rather than simply more complicated.

Interpretation

What strikes me as particularly unsettling is… I read this less as ___ and more as ___. The scene can be interpreted as… Orwell seems to blur the distinction between… This detail suggests / implies / reinforces…

Qualification & counterargument

That reading is persuasive, although… A possible counterargument would be… Up to a point, but the text also… I’m not sure the evidence fully supports… It would be reductive to see this simply as…

High-value vocabulary

internalise
to absorb a rule or belief until it regulates you from within
orthodoxy
the officially accepted set of beliefs
coercion
control through pressure, force, or threat
euphemism
a mild or misleading expression used in place of a harsher reality
retroactively
in a way that changes how an earlier event is treated or understood
epistemic
connected with knowledge, evidence, and what can count as true
